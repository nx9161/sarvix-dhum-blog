Blog-1 · Weeks 1, 2 & 3

When the Web Becomes Evidence

My first Digital Humanities reflection

One of the first ideas I am carrying into Digital Humanities is that the internet is not the same thing as a permanent record. It feels permanent because information is easy to copy, search, and share, but the readings for Weeks 1, 2, and 3 show how quickly digital evidence can disappear. A page can vanish when a company closes, a platform changes its policies, a server is not renewed, or a legal dispute changes what is allowed to remain online.

A history that can disappear

Chris Stokel-Walker’s BBC article begins with a statistic that made the problem concrete: research found that 25 percent of web pages posted between 2013 and 2023 had vanished, with older pages disappearing at an even higher rate. The article presents the Internet Archive and its Wayback Machine as an important response. Volunteers and archivists have built an enormous collection of snapshots, books, videos, and other digital materials so that researchers can still encounter pieces of the web that no longer exist in their original form.

What surprised me was how fragile that solution is. The Internet Archive is doing public-facing historical work, but it still depends on money, storage, technical infrastructure, volunteers, and legal permission. Cyberattacks and lawsuits can threaten the archive itself. That tension changed the way I think about “the cloud.” Digital information may look weightless, but it depends on physical data centers, institutions, and people willing to maintain it.

The article also made me think about what future historians will not be able to see. Much of our everyday life now happens through websites, social media, online communities, and digital services. If those spaces disappear without being preserved, the loss is not only technical. It changes the evidence available for understanding how people lived, communicated, organized, and made culture.

From scarcity to abundance

Roy Rosenzweig’s “Scarcity or Abundance? Preserving the Past in a Digital Era” approaches the same problem through history. His example of the “Bert Is Evil” website is memorable because it shows that even a strange piece of internet culture can become historical evidence. The site disappeared after its creator deleted it, yet copies and mirrors survived in other places. That story raises a difficult question: what counts as worth preserving, and who gets to decide?

Rosenzweig describes a shift from an older age of scarcity to a digital age of abundance. In the past, historians often worried that too few documents survived. In the digital era, we may face the opposite problem: too much information is produced too quickly, and much of it is poorly organized, undocumented, or dependent on software that may not exist in the future. Abundance does not automatically create better history. Researchers still need ways to select, describe, preserve, interpret, and teach from the record.

Rosenzweig’s argument also makes preservation feel less like a purely technical assignment. File formats and storage systems matter, but so do copyright, funding, public policy, institutional responsibility, and access. The Internet Archive is powerful precisely because it makes digital history available to the public, but its private and legally uncertain position shows why preservation cannot depend on one organization or one passionate founder alone.

Why this matters to a Data Science major

These readings connected directly to the way I think about datasets. A dataset can appear complete while still reflecting decisions about what was collected, labeled, excluded, or lost. A digital archive works the same way. Its search box may make a collection feel neutral, but the collection is shaped by crawl schedules, metadata, file formats, language support, permissions, and the choices of the people who built it.

The biggest takeaway for me is that preservation is also a question of representation. If a community’s websites, images, posts, or local histories are not archived, future researchers may mistake absence for unimportance. That is why Digital Humanities asks both technical and humanistic questions: How do we store a record, and whose record are we storing?

Digital practice I began: exploring the Wayback Machine

Alongside the readings, I began learning how web archiving works by exploring the Wayback Machine. The basic process is simple: enter a web address, choose an available date, and compare an archived snapshot with the live or missing page. The skill is not only clicking through old pages. It is learning to treat an archived page as evidence with a date, a capture history, and limitations.

The challenge is that an archive is never a perfect copy of the web. Images, scripts, links, and interactive features may be missing, and a page may have been captured only once. I managed that uncertainty by paying attention to the capture date and by treating the snapshot as one source rather than the entire story. If I taught this skill to another student, I would begin with a familiar website that has changed over time, then ask them to record what survived, what disappeared, and what questions the archive cannot answer.

What I am carrying forward

Weeks 1, 2, and 3 taught me that the digital past is threatened by both scarcity and abundance. Some records disappear before anyone saves them, while other records accumulate so quickly that future researchers may struggle to understand them. My responsibility as a student is not to assume that digital information will take care of itself. I need to ask how a source was created, how it was preserved, what context it lost, and who can still access it.

That question will stay with me as I continue studying Digital Humanities: when I find a digital source, am I only using it, or am I also helping understand its history?

Field note

Weeks 1, 2, and 3 made me think about how easily digital culture can disappear. I want to pay more attention to what gets preserved and what gets left behind.

Blog-2 · Week 4

The Search Box Is Not Neutral

My first Digital Humanities reflection

Before this week, I usually thought of digital tools as ways to make research faster. Search engines find information, databases organize it, maps display it, and code helps us process it. As a Data Science major, I am used to thinking about tools in terms of efficiency: How much information can I collect? How quickly can I find a pattern? How can I make a result easier to interpret?

The Week 4 readings made me slow down and question that mindset. Digital Humanities is not just about applying technology to humanities material. It is also about asking what technology changes, what it hides, and who gets represented when information becomes digital.

Digital Humanities as intervention

In the introduction to New Digital Worlds, Roopika Risam describes postcolonial Digital Humanities as an intervention in the “digital cultural record.” Her example of the Puerto Rico Mapathon made this idea concrete. After Hurricane Maria, students, university staff, and community members worked with OpenStreetMap to improve geographic information that could support disaster relief. This was not technology for technology’s sake. Mapping became a form of public service, collaboration, and teaching.

What stood out to me was the connection between theory, praxis, and pedagogy. Theory helps us understand the historical and political problems inside digital systems. Praxis means acting on those problems by building or improving tools, archives, maps, and workflows. Pedagogy means teaching other people how to participate. The Mapathon brought all three together: participants thought critically, performed technical work, and helped others learn the process.

Risam also argues that the digital cultural record contains gaps, omissions, and older colonial patterns. Digital projects can preserve and share knowledge, but they can also repeat the same inequalities found in older archives. If some languages, communities, and histories were underrepresented in the original cultural record, digitizing that record does not automatically make it fair. The process of choosing what to scan, how to describe it, how to organize it, and who can access it all matters.

That point connected strongly to my Data Science background. A dataset is never simply “the truth.” It reflects decisions about collection, categories, missing values, labels, and access. A digital archive has similar decisions built into it. The database may look objective because it is organized and searchable, but its structure still reflects human choices and institutional power.

The opportunities and blind spots of search

Lara Putnam’s article, “The Transnational and the Text-Searchable,” focuses on how digitized sources and full-text search have changed historical research. Search makes it possible to discover people, places, words, and connections across large collections without physically traveling to every archive. Putnam calls attention to the value of “side-glancing”: looking beyond the boundaries of a familiar topic or location to notice unexpected connections.

This is exciting to me as someone who works with data. Search increases the scale and speed of discovery. It can reveal patterns that would have been difficult or impossible to find through older research methods. Putnam explains that digital search has helped historians think across national borders because information is no longer as tightly connected to one physical place.

At the same time, the article warns that easier discovery can create new forms of ignorance. Search results are shaped by what has been digitized, what can be recognized by optical character recognition, what language the system handles well, and what terms a researcher already knows to enter. The sources that are easiest to find can begin to look like the most important sources, even when they are only the most searchable ones.

Digital search does not simply open a window onto the past. It creates a particular window, with a particular frame.

Putnam’s argument changed the way I think about a search box. It can help us see connections, but it can also make us overlook local context, people who left fewer written records, and communities whose materials were never digitized. In data terms, search is powerful, but it still has coverage problems, bias, and blind spots.

Digital skill I practiced: Hypothesis annotation

The new tool I practiced this week was Hypothesis, a web-based annotation tool. Instead of reading a PDF silently and keeping all of my reactions separate from the text, I could select a passage, highlight it, and attach a note directly to the relevant sentence. I could also read other annotations and see how classmates interpreted the same passage.

The basic workflow was simple: select text, choose Annotate, write a response, and then return to the page to read the conversation around it. The harder part was deciding what deserved an annotation. I became more intentional when I used three questions: What is the author claiming? What does this connect to? What do I want to question?

The biggest challenge was moving between close reading and the interface. Annotation adds another layer to reading because I was not only trying to understand the author’s argument; I was also trying to explain my own reaction clearly enough for someone else to follow. Reading classmates’ notes showed me that a passage can generate several legitimate questions. Annotation became less like finding one correct answer and more like making my thinking visible.

If I taught Hypothesis to another student, I would begin with a short passage and ask them to write one observation, one connection, and one question. I would also explain that a useful annotation does not have to be long. It should make a specific part of the reading easier to discuss.

What I am taking forward

My main takeaway from this week is that Digital Humanities is both technical and ethical. It asks us to build, search, map, annotate, and analyze, but it also asks us to examine the assumptions inside those actions. The goal is not simply to add more information to the internet. The goal is to create digital knowledge more carefully, with attention to context, representation, access, and power.

As a Data Science major, I am interested in the possibilities of computation. This week reminded me that computational skill is strongest when it is paired with humanistic questions. Before trusting a dataset, archive, search result, or visualization, I should ask: Who created it? What was left out? Which communities are represented? What would I fail to see if I only followed the easiest results?

That is the question I want to carry into the rest of this course and into the rest of my work with data: not only “What can this tool show me?” but also “What is this tool making harder to see?”

Field note

Week 4 made me slow down when I search. I now try to record my query, compare the results, and notice what the search leaves out.

Blog-3 · Week 5

Open tools should open the conversation.

My reflection on access, disability, and Digital Humanities

This week changed the way I think about the word open. Before these readings, I mostly connected openness with free access to an article, dataset, or software download. Helene Schlicht’s discussion of Open Science showed me that openness has several connected parts. Open Access, Open Data, Open Software, open review, open methods, and open metrics all ask a similar question: who can participate in the production and use of knowledge?

That question matters in Digital Humanities because DH projects depend on tools, platforms, archives, code, metadata, and public interfaces. A project can look innovative while still creating barriers through cost, license, format, documentation, or design. If a researcher needs an expensive subscription, inaccessible software, or technical knowledge that was never explained, the project is not equally open to everyone.

Openness is more than free access

Schlicht’s distinction between gratis and libre Open Access helped me make a more precise connection. Gratis access removes a price barrier, while libre access also addresses permission. A resource can be free to read and still restrict copying, adaptation, teaching, or reuse. This matters to me as a Data Science major because data and code are most useful when other people can inspect them, reproduce the process, improve the method, and build from the result.

The FAIR principles give this idea a practical structure: data should be findable, accessible, interoperable, and reusable. I can ask whether a dataset has clear documentation, whether another researcher can open the file, whether fields have understandable meanings, and whether permissions allow responsible reuse. Openness becomes a design decision and a research habit, not just a slogan.

Accessibility is a community issue

The reading on “Reframing the Conversation: Digital Humanists, Disabilities, and Accessibility” pushed this conversation further. Accessibility is often discussed as if disabled people are an audience outside the main project. The chapter challenges that assumption by showing that disabled people are already part of Digital Humanities communities as researchers, students, developers, teachers, presenters, and readers. Accessibility is not only a service provided to someone else. It is part of how the field includes its own members.

I found the distinction between accessibility and accommodation important. An accommodation responds to a specific need after a barrier has appeared. Accessibility tries to reduce the barrier in the design itself. Captions, transcripts, alt text, readable contrast, keyboard navigation, clear headings, descriptive links, and multiple ways to understand a visualization can help many people before anyone has to ask for special treatment.

The social model of disability changed how I read the examples. The problem can be a website that depends on color alone, a video without captions, a presentation with unreadable text, a map with no textual alternative, or documentation that assumes everyone already understands the tool. When the environment changes, participation can change too.

Why this matters to a Data Science major

These readings connected directly to how I think about data products. A model, dashboard, chart, map, or small application is never only a technical object. It creates a way for people to encounter information. If I use low contrast, confusing labels, color as the only signal, or a chart with no text explanation, I may make the data harder to access even if the underlying numbers are correct.

Accessibility also affects data collection. If a survey is not usable with assistive technology, some people may never enter the dataset. If a form assumes one kind of body, language, schedule, or device, missing responses can look like missing data when they are actually evidence of a design problem.

Open research asks who can use the work. Accessible design asks who can participate in the work.
Digital skill I practiced: building a TimelineJS story

For this week’s digital tool, I practiced using Knight Lab TimelineJS. TimelineJS turns a sequence of dates into a visual story that combines text, images, links, and media. I learned how a timeline can make change easier to understand because each event has a place in a larger sequence.

I started by identifying the key moments I wanted to show, then organized each event with a date, headline, short explanation, and media reference. The challenge was deciding how much information belonged in each event. Too much text makes the timeline difficult to follow, while too little text removes the context that gives an event meaning. If I taught this skill to another student, I would begin with a familiar personal timeline, ask them to choose five meaningful moments, and then show them how to revise each event into a clear combination of time, evidence, and reflection.

This skill connects to my StoryMap project because both tools ask me to turn experience into a sequence. The difference is that a map emphasizes location, while TimelineJS emphasizes change over time. For my own work, I want to use both carefully rather than treating a visual tool as decoration.

What I am taking forward

My main takeaway from Week 5 is that technical choices are also social choices. The platform I select, the license I use, the data format I save, the software I depend on, and the way I present a result all influence who can participate. Open Science gives me a language for access, reuse, and control. Disability and accessibility studies remind me that inclusion must be built into everyday practice.

As I continue studying Data Science and Digital Humanities, I want to become more careful about efficiency and participation. A fast tool is not automatically a good tool if it excludes people. A beautiful visualization is not successful if its meaning disappears for anyone who cannot see the colors.

Field note

Week 5 made me think about who can access, reuse, understand, and change a digital project. I want to ask those questions before I call a tool open.

Blog-4 · Week 6

Searchable does not mean complete.

My reflection on language, digital archives, and handwritten text recognition

I usually think of a digital tool as something that helps me get past a problem. These readings made me think more about the problems a tool can create, or carry forward, without making them obvious. Language support, the way an archive is organized, and the examples used to train a model all affect what becomes available to a reader. A searchable result is useful, but I still need to ask how it got there.

What happens when English becomes the default?

Paul Spence and Lorella Viola’s introduction to Multilingual Digital Humanities made me look differently at something I normally take for granted. English is so common in software, documentation, and academic work that it can start to feel like a neutral starting point. Their discussion shows why it is not. Researchers working with other languages may have fewer tools, less training material, or systems that do not handle their scripts well. That can limit the questions they are able to ask before the research even begins.

What stood out to me was that multilingual work means more than translating a page or adding another language to a menu. A tool also needs to work with the material, and the people using it need a way to contribute. If someone has to change their sources to fit the software, it is worth asking whether the software is supporting their work or making them compromise it.

I also appreciated that the authors acknowledge the contradiction of discussing multilingual DH in an English-language book. They are not claiming that including several languages solves every problem. They point out that support can still concentrate around a small group of well-resourced languages. Calling a project global does not tell me whose language it actually supports.

As a Data Science major, this makes me more careful about what a successful model result means. Good performance on English material does not automatically show that the method works equally well elsewhere. As an international student, I also want to notice when participation depends on adapting to one language or one way of working. I would rather check those assumptions at the beginning of a project than discover them after choosing all my tools.

Can something be in the archive and still be invisible?

The Global Debates in the Digital Humanities extract helped me see that missing information is not always information that was never collected. In Sayan Bhattacharyya’s discussion, words from non-Western languages can appear in several romanized spellings. A tool that counts each spelling separately may treat every version as too uncommon to include. The words are there, but the assumptions behind the analysis make them harder to see.

This is a useful challenge to how I think about cleaning data. My first instinct might be to standardize the spellings and move on. That could help in some cases, but I need to understand the variation before deciding that it is just noise. Bhattacharyya’s suggestion of keeping a record of the invisibilities students discover also appealed to me. Explaining what a method leaves out can be as useful as showing what it finds.

Puthiya Purayil Sneha brings a wider view through archival work in India. Her examples of institutional, community, and personal archives show why the history of DH cannot be reduced to one familiar story about American and European universities. I was especially interested in the work that continues after a collection is digitized. Describing materials, maintaining access, handling ownership and privacy, and keeping a project running are part of the archive too. An online collection does not sustain itself.

Ernesto Priego’s reflection connects this to who gets to participate. Blogging and inexpensive tools can open opportunities, but they do not give everyone the same time, funding, infrastructure, or recognition. His point about building a community alongside a resource feels relevant to this blog. Publishing something is not the same as making it meaningful to other people. Together, these chapters make me ask who helped create a digital collection, whose work gets recognized, and which experiences its categories might leave out.

What can AI tell us about an old handwritten page?

Aleksej Tikhonov and his coauthors begin the MultiHTR chapter with the possibility of finding old family letters and not being able to read their script. I liked this example because it gives the technology a purpose beyond producing a better accuracy score. Recognizing handwriting could help someone reconnect with a family history that is physically present but difficult to access. The project works with German, Russian, Serbian, and Ottoman Turkish, where changes in scripts and writing practices create different challenges.

The process also makes clear how much human work supports the AI. The researchers use carefully prepared transcriptions, called ground truth, to train models in Transkribus. They then compare the output with manually transcribed material held aside for validation. To me, that makes the result easier to understand: the model has learned from particular examples, not developed a complete understanding of every handwritten document.

One example that stayed with me was the Serbian chemistry notebook. The squared paper caused problems for layout analysis, and the model struggled with formulas it had not seen during training. Recognizing a script was not enough to handle a different kind of page. This connects directly to the way I think about testing models. Before trusting a result on new material, I need to check how that material differs from the training examples.

The discussion of Ottoman Turkish adds another important distinction. Someone trying to understand a family document broadly may need a different transcription from a linguist studying the exact historical characters. There is not one useful output for every reader. I need to know what the result will be used for before deciding whether its accuracy is good enough.

My takeaway is not that AI transcription should be avoided. It can make difficult material easier to approach, but the original page and the choices behind the transcription still matter. If I used a model for this kind of work, I would keep its output connected to the source and make uncertainty clear. That brings me back to the questions running through these readings: who can use the tool, what does it make visible, and what do I still need to check for myself?

Digital skill I practiced: organizing a research collection with Tropy

For this week’s digital tool, I practiced using Tropy, a free tool built for organizing research photographs and the information attached to them. I built a small practice collection of five landscape paintings from The Met’s Open Access collection and described each one with the same fields: title, creator, date, identifier, source, and rights. The deck in the Tropy section below this post shows the collection and how it is organized.

The slow part was not adding the photographs. It was deciding how to describe them the same way every time. I used lists to separate the items I had reviewed from the ones still to describe, and tags such as landscape and open-access to keep the collection searchable. A field left blank, or filled in two different ways across items, becomes a small inconsistency that only grows as a collection does.

This practice connects directly to the week’s readings. The chapters argue that systems decide what becomes visible. In Tropy, that stopped being abstract: the fields I chose decided what could be found later, and anything I failed to describe stayed in the collection but out of reach.

Field note

Week 6 made me question what looks like a default: the language a tool supports, the categories an archive uses, and the material a model learns from. I want to check those choices before treating a digital result as the whole story.

Blog-5 · Week 7

Automatic does not mean human-free.

My reflection on human-centred AI, the hidden labour behind ChatGPT, and reading a text without reading it

So far this semester I have mostly asked what digital tools make visible and what they leave out. This week the readings turned that question toward the people inside the system. One article argues that humanities students should help design AI. The other shows workers who already shape AI every day, in ways most users never see. The tool for the week, Voyant, sits in between: it gives more people a way to analyse text, while still depending on choices someone made before the analysis starts.

Who should learn how AI works?

Jon Chun and Katherine Elkins start from crisis: humanities enrolments falling, students using ChatGPT to write, and AI moving into white-collar work. Their answer is not retreat. They built an AI curriculum inside digital humanities at Kenyon College so that humanities students can help create what they call human-centred AI. One question runs through the article: will we program humanity into our tools, or will we cede our humanity to AI?

What convinced me is that they do not stop at critique. They warn that critique alone sidelines humanists from decisions being made without them. Their courses teach real tools, including Python, Pandas, scikit-learn, and Google Colab. Students examine bias at every stage of the data science pipeline, then test competing definitions of fairness on the COMPAS parole algorithm using the What-If Tool. Fairness cannot be settled by one equation. Someone has to choose which definition to use, and that is a humanities question as much as a technical one.

As a Data Science major, I am usually on the other side of this classroom. My courses go deep on how models work and lighter on who decides what they are for. Their enrolment figures stayed with me: 61 percent women and more than 90 percent non-STEM students, in courses built entirely on free tools with no textbook costs. Access is a design choice. The important questions are shifting from how a model works to who it serves and why, and I want both kinds of fluency.

Who reads the worst of the internet so a chatbot stays polite?

Billy Perrigo’s TIME investigation shows the other side of that classroom. To make ChatGPT less toxic, OpenAI sent tens of thousands of snippets of text to an outsourcing firm, Sama, in Kenya, beginning in November 2021. Workers read and labelled graphic descriptions of sexual abuse, hate speech, and violence so that a safety system could learn to detect toxic language and filter it out.

The numbers are hard to sit with. Labelers took home between about $1.32 and $2 per hour, while OpenAI paid Sama $12.50 per hour for the project. Workers told TIME they were expected to read and label 150 to 250 passages in a nine-hour shift; Sama says the figure was 70. All four workers interviewed described being mentally scarred by the material, and the wellness support they were promised was hard to reach in practice. In February 2022, Sama cancelled the work eight months early, after a related image project raised alarms about illegal content. Ending the contract ended the exposure. It also cost workers income, and some of them their jobs.

In my field, labels like these are called ground truth, the same term from last week’s handwriting recognition project. A safety model is trained on human judgment, made quickly, under pressure, by people far from the product launch. When I evaluate a classifier I usually ask about its accuracy. This article makes me ask about the conditions its labels were produced under, and whose judgment the model has actually learned. Less toxic output rested on people absorbing the toxicity first.

What can you learn from a text without reading it?

Voyant Tools is this week’s digital tool: a free, browser-based way to analyse text through word frequencies, a word cloud of the most common terms, trends across a document, and words shown in context. It needs no code, which makes it exactly the kind of low-barrier tool Chun and Elkins describe. A humanities student can run a genuine analysis in minutes.

From my text mining coursework, I know what Voyant is doing under the surface: splitting text into tokens, counting them, and comparing the counts. Those steps carry assumptions. The romanized spelling variants from last week’s readings would be counted as separate words, so part of the pattern Voyant shows is a product of how the text was split before counting began. Distant reading reveals shapes no reader could see across thousands of pages. It cannot tell me what a passage means.

Used together, close and distant reading check each other. That is my running lesson from this course, now with a new layer. Every tool I have used this semester has people inside it: the people who designed it, the people whose labour trained it, and the people whose texts it counts. Before I trust a result, I still ask who can use the tool and what it makes visible. Now I also ask whose work is hidden inside it.

Digital skill I practiced: analyzing a text with Voyant Tools

For this week’s digital tool, I practiced using Voyant Tools, a free text analysis environment that runs in the browser. I loaded the full text of this week’s TIME investigation, cleaned of the PDF’s page headers and link text, and let Voyant count it: 2,998 words, 1,014 of them unique. The full session, with screenshots, is in the Voyant section below this post.

The first surprise was the ranking. The most frequent word was not OpenAI, at 41, but Sama, at 49, the outsourcing firm in the middle of the story. Around the two names sat a workplace vocabulary: work, content, employees, images. The counts alone were not the finding, though. Reading the sixteen appearances of “workers” in context showed people mostly being acted on: employed, asked, split into teams, moved. The count said how often. The contexts said how.

The limits mattered as much as the results. “Toxic” appears only six times in an article that is entirely about toxicity, and “labour”, the British spelling, appears zero times, so a spelling choice quietly decides what the tool can find. It reminded me of the spelling variants in last week’s readings: every count is decided before the counting starts, by the cleaning and the rules. I left with the same habit I am building all semester. Check who a tool centers, and read a few contexts before trusting any number it gives me.

Field note

Week 7 shifted my attention from what a tool shows to who stands behind it: the students Chun and Elkins want in the room, and the Kenyan workers Perrigo shows were never in it. I want to keep asking both questions: who is invited to shape AI, and who is already shaping it without being seen.
